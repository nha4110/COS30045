READ THE MEASURE

Rates are not headcounts.

Rates adjust for population size. Pew’s U.S. trend uses adults; the international map uses residents of all ages. Country data years also vary. Those rates should not be compared as if they were one identical dataset.

DESIGN REVIEW

Reading the map

Exercise 7.1 · Visualisation 2

What appears to be the purpose of the chart?

To show where incarceration rates stand out geographically and to support Pew's point that the U.S. rate, though falling, remained the highest among countries with available data. It invites comparison between the U.S. and a few high-rate countries (El Salvador, Turkmenistan, Rwanda, Cuba) rather than a full global ranking.

What kind of chart is this?

A choropleth world map: each country is a polygon filled according to its incarceration-rate band. The scale is stepped (binned) rather than a continuous gradient, and countries without a represented rate use a neutral fill.

What data does it encode, and how?

Rates per 100,000 residents of any age, for about 177 countries. Country area is the mark, geographic position encodes location, and fill color encodes the rate in eight ordered bins (0–99 up to 600+, plus “no national rate”). Only nine values are Pew figures (e.g. U.S. 639, El Salvador 564); the rest are clearly labelled illustrative mock values.

Are there interactive features?

Yes. Hovering or keyboard-focusing a country shows a tooltip with its rate and data note, and the nine Pew countries are keyboard focusable. Region buttons (World, North America, Europe, Asia, etc.) zoom the map and list every country in that region with its value, and clicking a country zooms to its region. Tabs switch between the map, the full country table, and a top-five-by-region bar chart with exact values.

What features help get its message across?

A graduated legend with an explicit unit, white country borders for contrast, and neutral gray for missing data keep the pattern readable. Direct labels with leader lines for the five highest Pew countries point to the key comparison, and source notes disclose mixed data years and the illustrative mock values so the map is not over-read.

Which design do you think is best and why?

The map is best for “where” — the geographic pattern (high rates in the U.S., parts of Latin America, Central Asia and southern Africa) is immediately visible. But color bins make exact values hard to compare, so for ranking and judging differences the sorted bar chart is stronger; together they beat either design alone, which is why the page pairs them.
